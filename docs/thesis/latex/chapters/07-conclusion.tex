\section{What the thesis shows}
\label{sec:conclusion-shows}

The thesis set out to show that payment disputes under different regulatory regimes do not need different
software: that one finite state machine, parameterised by the regime, can carry PSD2 card disputes, SEPA
direct-debit claims, Regulation~E debit-card errors and Regulation~Z billing errors. The engine carries all four.
Its 15 states are shared, and each regime reaches the part of them its rules describe: 6 under SEPA, 11
under PSD2, 13 under Regulation~E and 10 under Regulation~Z. None of the differences is written as a test of
a regime's name; each is a property of the rules. Every one of the 64 transitions in the four regimes' tables is
walked by a named scenario that ends in a closed dispute, and every pair of state and event a regime forbids is
refused (section~\ref{sec:eval-correctness}).

A lifecycle modelled this way also carries the rest of an issuer's process. Deadline compliance can be read at any
moment rather than assembled afterwards; the ledger's suspense account is zero whenever a dispute closes; the
database rather than the code keeps tenants apart; and the analyst who investigated a dispute cannot issue its
final credit. Each of these is checked by a test, against the real database wherever the database is what
enforces it (sections~\ref{sec:eval-log} and~\ref{sec:eval-isolation}).

\section{What the decisions cost}
\label{sec:conclusion-costs}

Several decisions bought correctness at a measured price. Taking each authorisation decision inside the request's
transaction doubled the median latency of a read, from 2.1 to 4.2 milliseconds in the September runs that isolate it; the cost is extra
round trips rather than the policy engine itself, which shows where to recover it without moving the decision
(section~\ref{sec:eval-latency}). Calling the banking core inside the transaction makes the engine as slow as the
slowest core it talks to, in exchange for never recording a refund the core did not make. Keeping PostgreSQL as
the only stateful dependency puts every counter, session and outbox beside the disputes; its queries stayed well
under a millisecond at this load.

Other decisions limited what the system claims rather than what it does. The fraud rules' weights are set by judgement
rather than fitted to data, and the rules may only hold a credit, never deny a dispute. The decision model is offered only behind a
confidence gate and was measured on a synthetic set its author labelled. The banking core is a simulator, and the
card network's answers are entered by hand; these limits are collected in section~\ref{sec:limitations}. The two
parts marked in the project plan as the first to cut, the fraud-scoring engine and the workbench, were both kept,
in a form chosen to be defensible rather than impressive.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:conclusion-future}

The contested rounds of a chargeback, such as pre-arbitration and arbitration, would become states of their own
with deadlines from the card network's rules, and the statutory extensions of deadlines would become clocks that an
event may extend. Both are additions to the rules rather than to the machine, which is the test the design was
meant to pass.

The next regulatory step is already visible. The European Union's proposed Payment Services Regulation would
extend the issuer's liability to some payments a customer was deceived into authorising, such as those made to a
fraudster impersonating the bank. A dispute may then arise from an authorised payment, and the question becomes
whether the bank's own fraud controls met the expected standard. That is a new regime in the sense of this thesis,
and a test of whether the existing gates are enough for a fifth.

Integration would turn the simulated core into an adapter for a real one, whose behaviour when slow or silent the
port was designed for but has not met; the card network would receive the chargebacks the engine now only records,
and its answers would become the events the analyst now enters by hand. Finally, two measurements the running
system already records would settle open questions: the share of the decision model's proposals that analysts
keep, recorded for the reason proposed when a dispute opens, and the outcomes of disputes, which are the labelled
data a learned fraud model would need to be compared against the fixed rules.
