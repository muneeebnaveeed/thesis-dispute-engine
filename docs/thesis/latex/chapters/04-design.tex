\section{The regime-parameterised state machine}
\label{sec:design-state-machine}

Every strand of a dispute depends on where the dispute currently stands: a clock is met by entering a state, a
posting is caused by entering a state, a notice is owed because a state was entered, and an authorization policy
decides on the state the dispute is in. The lifecycle is therefore the spine of the design, and the first decision
was how to model it when four regimes disagree about what a lifecycle contains. Instead of a workflow per regime,
the engine has a single state machine whose shape is shared and whose regime decides which parts of it exist.

The shared lifecycle has fifteen states (figure~\ref{fig:state-machine}). A dispute is \emph{initiated}, an
analyst opens the \emph{investigation}, and the customer may be sent a questionnaire and answer it. The
customer may then receive money in one of three forms, which are separate states because they are different
things in law: a \emph{provisional credit} that can be taken back, a \emph{fast refund} that is final, and a
\emph{no-questions-asked refund} of a direct debit. The bank may then file a chargeback, which the merchant may
answer with evidence before the network rules it won or lost. A provisional credit is then made final or
reversed. Every path ends in \emph{closed}, and a closed dispute can be appealed, which reopens the investigation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{state-machine.pdf}
\caption{The dispute lifecycle under all four regimes, drawn from the transition table by a test

and allowed once, is not drawn.}
\label{fig:state-machine}
\end{figure}

What differs between regimes is expressed as a handful of properties of the regime's rules, never as a test
of the regime's name: the kind of money the customer receives (none, a no-questions-asked refund, a fast refund
or a provisional credit), whether the regime has adjudication at all, and whether the merchant may contest. The
transition function asks these questions and nothing else. A SEPA claim can be refunded straight from
initiation; a credit-card dispute under Regulation~Z files the chargeback directly from the investigation,
because there is no refund to come first; a won chargeback under Regulation~E cannot close until the
provisional credit is made final, while under PSD2 the refund was final all along. The regimes therefore reach
different parts of the same machine: six states under SEPA, eleven under PSD2, thirteen under Regulation~E and
ten under Regulation~Z. A fifth regime would need a new row of rules, its entry in the list of regimes, the
branch that selects it and its tests, but no new code in the machine. The regime is fixed when the dispute
opens, from the transaction's rail and the account's currency (table~\ref{tab:regimes}), so a dispute cannot
drift from one set of rules to another.

The machine is one pure function from a dispute and an event to the next dispute, written as a table of cases
rather than with a library, which would have needed a machine per regime or guard callbacks that hide the table.
A refused event is an invalid-transition error, reported as a \code{409} problem (section~\ref{sec:design-failures}),
and the workbench's buttons are the events the same function accepts. The appeal is the one transition outside the
table: allowed only from \emph{closed}, at most once, it starts a new resolution clock. The tests are reported in
section~\ref{sec:eval-correctness}.

\section{Regulatory clocks}
\label{sec:design-clocks}

A dispute must reach particular states by particular dates, from PSD2's refund by the end of the next business
day \cite[art.~73(1)]{psd2} to Regulation~Z's resolution within ninety days \cite[\S~1026.13(c)]{regz}
(table~\ref{tab:regimes}). Three of the four regimes count business days for at least one clock, which depends on
the jurisdiction's holidays. An engine that treats deadlines as documentation leaves the analyst to compute them,
and the institution unable to say how often it met them.

Each deadline is therefore a \emph{clock} on the regime's rules (appendix, table~\ref{tab:clocks}): a kind, a
count, a unit and the citation. Each of the three kinds is defined by what satisfies it rather than by the
regime: the refund clock is met by entering any credit state, the acknowledgement clock by sending the
acknowledgement notice, the resolution clock by closing. A clock that can no longer be met, such as the refund
clock of a dispute closed without a credit, is voided rather than breached, and an appeal starts a new
resolution clock while the history keeps the old one.

Because a due date is something the institution has told the customer, it is computed once, when the dispute
opens, and stored beside the dispute; a later change to a rule or a holiday calendar cannot silently move it.
The calendar is tenant data rather than code: a tenant's settings name its time zone and public holidays, and
the unit tests in \code{domain/deadline\_test.go} fix weekends, holidays and time zones. Status, on the other
hand, is never stored: whether a clock is \emph{running}, \emph{met}, \emph{late}, \emph{breached} or \emph{void} is
a comparison with the current time, so nothing has to run at midnight to notice a breach, and the workbench's filter,
the API and the overdue gauge all ask the same question of the same rows.

\section{The banking core adapter}
\label{sec:design-core}

The ledger says what should move; a bank's core system moves it. What matters is the port between the engine and
whatever core a tenant has. An \emph{instruction} is one movement on the customer's account;
a \emph{receipt} answers in ISO 8583's vocabulary, with a retrieval reference (field 37), a response code
(field 39) and whether the core recognised the reference as one it had already carried out (code 94, duplicate
transmission). Only postings that touch the customer's account cross the port. Each tenant names its core in its
settings, and the simulator behind the port (\code{infrastructure/mockcore}) can be told per tenant to decline, to
be slow or to be down; the two seed tenants differ on purpose, one refusing credits above a limit and one
answering slowly.

The decision that shapes everything else is \emph{when} to call. The engine calls the core inside the
transition's own database transaction, with a bounded timeout. A decline rolls the transition back and returns a
\code{422} problem carrying the core's response code; silence rolls it back as a \code{503} that the client repeats
with the same idempotency key. Because the transition was never written, the repeat computes the same posting
reference, and a core that did move the money before its answer was lost recognises the reference and answers
duplicate instead of paying twice. The alternative, an outbox with a reconciliation job, frees the engine from the
core's latency at the price of a window in which the dispute and the core disagree about whether the customer was
paid. For a system whose job is to be right about that, holding the lock is the cheaper price: a slow core slows
its own dispute and no other, and \code{TestARetryAfterSilenceIsADuplicateNotADoublePayment} holds the retry to
its promise.

\section{The ledger}
\label{sec:design-ledger}

Three of the four regimes move the customer's money before anyone knows who was right. The original proposal
imagined separate provisional-credit and final-credit engines; both disappear into one small ledger once the
question becomes ``where is the money now?'' rather than ``what kind of credit is this?''. The ledger has four
accounts from the issuer's point of view (appendix, table~\ref{tab:ledger}): the customer's account, a suspense
account holding what the bank has advanced and not yet cleared, and recovery and loss, which clear it. Every
posting is double-entry and caused by a transition: entering a credit state debits suspense and credits the
customer, a reversal does the opposite, a won chargeback recovers, a lost one writes off unless a provisional
credit is reversed instead, and closing settles whatever remains. Again, the postings follow from the state
entered and two properties of the rules, never from the regime's name.

The property that matters is that suspense is zero when a dispute closes. It is not checked at close; it holds by
construction, and a test proves it by enumerating every path from \code{INITIATED} to \code{CLOSED} under every
regime, forty-three at the time of writing, replaying each with each settlement choice, and asserting a zero
balance and a customer never left net debited (\code{TestSuspenseBalancesOnEveryPath}). Postings are append-only rows, each carrying the reference the banking core adapter uses as its idempotency key,
and the amount credited is the disputed amount less a customer liability capped by the regime.

\section{Fraud scoring}
\label{sec:design-fraud}

Fraud scoring was planned as one of the first things to cut if time ran short, and it survived in a deliberately modest
form. A learned model needs labelled outcomes the project does not have, and where a customer has a right to know
why they were refused, a score nobody can explain is a liability. The engine therefore scores with seven fixed,
readable signals, each reporting a sentence that says why it scored what it did: the account's other disputes in
twelve months, the disputes it lost, the delay since the transaction, the merchant's category, the amount, the
account's age, and the contradictions in the questionnaire (section~\ref{sec:design-questionnaire}). The score is
the sum, and two thresholds divide it into three tiers.

The score is assessed when the dispute opens and again when the questionnaire arrives, and every assessment is
kept. Its only power is a hold: a credit on a dispute whose latest tier is high is refused until the analyst
records why it should go out anyway. The score never denies a dispute or moves a state. The weights are judgement, not fitted to data, and chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} treats them as a
demonstration of the mechanism rather than a claim about fraud.

\section{The questionnaire}
\label{sec:design-questionnaire}

The questionnaire is where the customer's claim becomes structured data. A dispute has an immutable
\emph{reason} from the moment it opens (unauthorised transaction, goods or services not received, duplicate
charge, or a different amount), and the reason selects the question set. The questions are \emph{snapshotted}
onto the dispute when sent, so an answer is always read against the exact question the customer saw, whatever
later changes. Answers are \emph{typed facts} (yes or no, a date, an amount, or free text), validated against the
snapshot with every problem reported per question, and the transition is refused as a whole when any answer is
wrong. A contradiction check runs over the answers (a customer who recognises the merchant, held the card and shared it
with nobody is not describing an unauthorised payment), and its findings become one of the fraud signals.

\section{The contract and its error taxonomy}
\label{sec:design-failures}

The engine is written in Go and the workbench in TypeScript; what they must share is an agreement about which
requests exist, what their bodies look like and what each failure means. That agreement lives in one OpenAPI
document. The server's interface is generated from it, so a route not in the document does not compile, and
every request is validated against it before a handler runs; the client's types, request functions and form
schemas are generated from the same document. The build fails if regenerating would change anything, so neither
side can drift for longer than one failed check.

Failures get the same treatment, because a client that reads error messages to decide what to do will eventually
get it wrong. Every failure has a \emph{kind}, one of nine classes that decides the HTTP status and whether a
retry can help, and a stable \emph{code}, one of 28 the contract lists. One table maps kind to status for the whole
service. The response is a problem document in the standard format \cite{rfc9457}, extended with the code, a
retry hint, a request reference and, for validation failures, one entry per field (figure~\ref{fig:problem}). A
refused transition lists the events the dispute does allow. Diagnostic context stays in the logs and the trace,
and tests check that it never leaks into a body and that the contract and the Go code list the same codes.

\section{Multi-tenancy with row-level security}
\label{sec:design-tenancy}

The engine serves several institutions from one deployment, and they must never share data without the design
depending on every query remembering that. A database or schema per tenant isolates well but multiplies
everything an operator runs by the number of customers. The design keeps one schema in which every row names its
tenant, and puts the enforcement in the database with PostgreSQL's row-level security \cite{pgrls}. Every
business table has the same policy: a row is visible and writable only when its tenant is the one bound to the
current transaction (figure~\ref{fig:rls}). The service binds the tenant from the authenticated caller, never
from the request, and only for that transaction, so a pooled connection cannot carry one tenant into another's
request. When nothing is bound the policy matches no rows, so mistakes fail closed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL]
CREATE FUNCTION current_tenant_id() RETURNS uuid LANGUAGE sql STABLE AS $$
    SELECT NULLIF(current_setting('app.tenant_id', true), '')::uuid
$$;
CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON disputes
    USING (tenant_id = current_tenant_id())
    WITH CHECK (tenant_id = current_tenant_id());

-- at the start of every transaction (Store.WithTx), local to that transaction
SELECT set_config('app.tenant_id', $1, true);
\end{lstlisting}
\caption{The tenant policy, here on \code{disputes} (\code{migrations/0003\_tenants.sql}), and the binding the
store makes before any query. An unbound setting yields \code{NULL}, which matches no rows.}
\label{fig:rls}
\end{figure}

The service runs as a role subject to the policies; the few things it needs across tenants, such as dashboard
counts and purges, are views and functions owned by the schema's owner. The price is that every new table needs
its tenant column, its policy and its grants; a test of the service role's grants catches a missing grant, while
the column and the policy are a review rule.

The tenant therefore has to come from the credential, and there are two kinds of caller
(figure~\ref{fig:credentials}). A tenant's own systems present a \emph{tenant key}, a random secret shown once and
kept only as a hash, which names its tenant and can be revoked on its own. Analysts sign in through an identity
provider \cite{oidc} in which every tenant has its own realm, so each institution's users are separate at the
level of the identity system; the tenant's short name in the workbench's address chooses the realm. The engine
verifies an analyst's token itself and decides the tenant from its issuer. The browser never holds either credential
(section~\ref{sec:impl-sessions}), and isolation is tested against the role the service actually uses
(section~\ref{sec:eval-isolation}).

\section{Authorization}
\label{sec:design-authorization}

Row-level security says which tenant a row belongs to, not who inside a tenant may do what. An issuer's
operations assume that work belongs to teams, that seniority bounds the amounts a person may settle alone, and
that the person who investigated a claim does not also make its final credit. The engine therefore decides every operation
that changes a dispute or the tenant with one policy engine, over one notion of the caller: a \emph{principal},
either an analyst named by their token or a tenant key. The actor on every event is this principal and never a
field of the request, so the audit trail records who acted rather than who claimed to.

Policies are written in Cedar \cite{cedar2024} and evaluated inside the service, chosen for three properties: it
denies by default, any policy that forbids an action overrides every policy that permits it, and its semantics are
formally specified, so a policy set can be checked against a schema before it runs. The engine splits its
policies along the second property. \emph{Guardrails} live in the binary where no tenant can change them, and are
mostly prohibitions: whoever opened the investigation never issues its final credit, only a team lead may move a
dispute elsewhere, and a tenant key never writes to a customer. The one permit gives tenant administration to the
realm role, and it is exclusive only because grants never compile to tenant resources. \emph{Grants} are data, one
row per team, role and action, optionally bounded by an amount, compiled into permissions that reach only their
own team's disputes; a grant that cannot be expressed safely is dropped. Because a prohibition always wins, no
combination of grants can open what a guardrail closes, which is what will later let tenants edit their own grants.

The decision is made inside the request's transaction, on committed facts: the dispute's team, amount, rail and
state, and who opened its investigation according to the event log. A refusal names the policy that decided it,
an allowed event records the policies that allowed it, and the workbench offers only actions that would pass.

\subsection{Teams, grants and a second line}
\label{sec:design-teams}

A dispute belongs to one team, chosen when it opens by the first routing rule matching its rail, reason and
amount. Inside a team a person holds one rung of a ladder, junior, senior or lead, expressed in Cedar's own
hierarchy so that a grant to juniors reaches every rung above without role logic in code. Generated sets of arbitrary grants confirm the three properties that matter: no set lets an investigator issue
the final credit of their own dispute, reach a dispute outside the caller's teams, or administer the tenant.

Team scope is enforced twice. Row-level security also narrows every read to the caller's teams, so a check
forgotten in a handler still cannot show one team another's work. These policies are restrictive rather than
permissive, because PostgreSQL combines permissive policies with \emph{or}, and a permissive team policy beside
tenant isolation would have widened access rather than narrowed it \cite{pgrls}. The one move the row policies cannot express, reassigning a dispute to another team, runs as the owner after the
policy engine has allowed it. The cost of deciding on
committed facts, extra round trips per request, is measured in section~\ref{sec:eval-latency}.

\section{Communications}
\label{sec:design-communications}

The regimes say when the bank must speak: Regulation~Z wants a written acknowledgement within thirty days
\cite[\S~1026.13(c)(1)]{regz}; Regulation~E wants the customer told of a provisional credit
\cite[\S~1005.11(c)(2)(ii)]{rege}, of the date and amount of a reversal \cite[\S~1005.11(d)(2)]{rege}, and of the
outcome \cite[\S~1005.11(d)(1)]{rege}. The engine keeps this as a function from the state entered to the notices it
triggers, sent by email and, where the regime requires writing, as a printable letter.

The design question is again \emph{when}. A transition must not be undone because a mail relay was down, but an
owed notice must not be lost if the process dies between the commit and the send. The engine therefore composes
the notice inside the transition, from facts it already holds, as structured paragraphs together with the
provision the notice satisfies, and stores it as a row in the same transaction. A letter is complete the moment
it exists. An email is an entry in
an outbox that a dispatcher drains after every commit and on a timer, which makes delivery at-least-once. The
acknowledgement clock of section~\ref{sec:design-clocks} is met by this notice rather than by a state, because the
acknowledgement is what the regulation asks for, and golden files pin the paragraphs of every notice under each
regime, so a change in what a customer reads is a reviewed diff.

Analysts also write on their own initiative, through the same machinery (ADR~0019): templates kept as data, with
a deliberately tiny substitution language. The API returns each template with the engine's facts filled in, the
browser previews the message by the same substitution as the analyst types, and the server renders the final
message on send. The substitution is written twice, once for each side, and no test yet makes the two equal, so
preview and letter agree by construction rather than by proof.
