\section{The problem}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

A payment dispute begins with a customer who objects to a line on their statement: a charge they
do not recognise, a purchase that never arrived, a subscription taken twice, or an amount larger
than the one they agreed to. They take it to their own bank rather than the shop, because the law
gives them rights against that bank that it does not give them against the shop.

For the bank the complaint starts several strands at once: legal deadlines, some measured in
business days; in several regimes, a duty to give the money back before anyone knows whether the
complaint is justified; a process on the other side of the card network to recover that money
from the merchant's bank; letters the customer is owed at particular points; and the money itself,
which must move in the bank's core system exactly once, and move back if the law allows and the
outcome requires it. Each strand is simple in isolation, but all depend on where the dispute
currently stands, and a mistake in any of them is a breach of a legal duty or a loss.

First-party fraud, often called friendly fraud, complicates this further: a dispute raised in bad
faith, or in error, by the legitimate cardholder, such as the parcel that did arrive. The
regulation assumes good faith and frequently requires the refund to come first, yet some disputes
are not what they claim to be. The position this thesis takes is that the customer is
given the benefit of the doubt at the start, with the refund or credit the law requires, and the
evidence then decides how the dispute ends.

\section{Why disputes are regime-specific}
\label{sec:intro-regimes}

The rules depend on the payment rail and the jurisdiction, and differ in more than their numbers.
Under the European Payment Services Directive, an unauthorised card payment is refunded by the end
of the next business day \cite{psd2}. A SEPA direct debit can be claimed back within 8 weeks
with no reason given and no defence available to the merchant \cite{sepacore}. The United States'
Regulation~E, for debit cards, requires a provisional credit while the bank investigates
\cite{rege}, and Regulation~Z, for credit cards, issues no credit at all and suspends collection of
the disputed amount \cite{regz}. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} sets these regimes out.

Software for several regimes can treat each as its own workflow, so that a fix in one is not a fix
in the others, or express their differences as configuration of one shared lifecycle. This thesis
argues for the second: a single finite state machine, parameterised by the regulatory regime, in
which the regime decides which states are reachable, which deadlines run and which money moves.
The system is written from the side of the issuer, the customer's own bank. The two European
regimes are the focus; the two American ones are modelled to show that the model generalises.

\section{What was built}
\label{sec:intro-built}

The result is a dispute engine offered as a service to several institutions, whose data the
database itself keeps apart, with an analyst workbench in front of it. The engine gates the
lifecycle by regime, runs the regulatory clocks in each institution's business-day calendar, posts
every movement of money to a ledger and through a port to the core banking system, collects the
customer's version of events through a questionnaire, sends the notices the customer is owed, and scores
each dispute with readable rules that can hold a credit until a person justifies it
(chapter~\ref{ch:design}). Its claims are meant to be checkable: tests walk every transition each
regime allows and refuse the ones it forbids, isolation is tested against the database, and every
number in the evaluation comes from a committed measurement run that one command repeats
(chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).
