\section{The parties to a dispute}
\label{sec:bg-parties}

A card payment involves four parties and an intermediary. The \emph{cardholder} pays; the
\emph{merchant} is paid; the \emph{issuer} is the cardholder's bank, which issued the card and holds
the account or extends the credit; the \emph{acquirer} is the merchant's bank, which accepts card
payments on the merchant's behalf. Between issuer and acquirer sits the \emph{card network}, which
routes the messages, sets the scheme rules, including those for disputes, and settles the
amounts between the banks, but holds no customer accounts of its own. A direct debit has the same
shape without the network: the payer's bank and the payee's bank exchange the debit and any
claim against it through the payment scheme.

A dispute engine can sit on either side of this picture, and the two sides do opposite things.
Merchant-side tools receive a chargeback that has already been raised and help the merchant
defend it with evidence. The system described in this thesis sits with the issuer. It receives
the customer's complaint, carries the legal duties that complaint triggers, decides whether and
when to refund or credit the customer, and, where the regime allows, raises the chargeback
outward through the network to recover the money. Every regime below is therefore read for what
it requires of the issuer.

\section{The European regimes}
\label{sec:bg-eu}

In the European Union the payer's rights against their bank come from the second Payment Services
Directive (PSD2) \cite{psd2}. A payer who notices an unauthorised or incorrectly executed payment
must report it without undue delay and at most thirteen months after the debit
\cite[art.~71(1)]{psd2}. For an unauthorised payment the bank must then refund the amount
immediately, and in any event by the end of the following business day \cite[art.~73(1)]{psd2}.
The only exception is a reasonable suspicion of fraud by the payer, which the bank must report to
the national authority in writing; it is not a licence to investigate first and refund later.
The payer's own share of the loss is capped at fifty euro, except where they acted fraudulently
or with gross negligence \cite[art.~74(1)]{psd2}. Whatever the outcome, the bank must answer the
customer's complaint in writing within fifteen business days \cite[art.~101(2)]{psd2}. There is no
notion of a provisional credit in this regime: the refund is the customer's, and if the bank
wants the money back it must recover it from the other side, through the card network.

Direct debits add a right that has no counterpart elsewhere. For a payment the payer did
authorise, PSD2 gives a right to a refund when the payment was initiated by the payee, and for a
SEPA direct debit that right is unconditional \cite[art.~76(1)]{psd2}: within eight weeks of the debit
\cite[art.~77(1)]{psd2} the payer can claim it back without giving a reason, and the scheme gives
the merchant no defence \cite{sepacore}. The bank must refund, or justify a refusal, within ten
business days of the request \cite[art.~77(2)]{psd2}. An unauthorised debit remains claimable for
thirteen months, as above. The two periods answer different questions: eight weeks is how long
the customer has to ask, and ten business days is how long the bank has to act. For the design
the important point is the absence of adjudication. A SEPA claim inside the eight weeks has no
evidence step, no chargeback and no possible defeat, and so takes the shortest path any dispute
can take.

\section{The US regimes, for comparison}
\label{sec:bg-us}

Two American regulations cover the same ground for debit and credit cards, and they are included
because they differ from the European regimes, and from each other, in ways that test whether one
state machine can carry them. Regulation~E governs debit cards and other electronic transfers.
The bank has ten business days to investigate; if it needs longer, up to forty-five days, it must
provisionally credit the customer within the first ten and tell them so
\cite[\S~1005.11(c)]{rege}. The customer's liability for an unauthorised transfer is limited, to
fifty dollars where a lost or stolen card is reported promptly \cite[\S~1005.6]{rege}. The
provisional credit is exactly that: if the investigation finds no error, the bank may take it
back after notice.

Regulation~Z governs credit cards and takes yet another approach \cite{regz}. The issuer must
acknowledge a billing-error notice in writing within thirty days and resolve it within two
billing cycles, and at most ninety days \cite[\S~1026.13(c)]{regz}. Nothing is credited while the
question is open. Instead the issuer may not collect the disputed amount or report it as
delinquent until the dispute is resolved. A credit-card dispute therefore has no refund state to
reach at all. These two regimes are not the market the system is built for; they are there to
show that the model is general rather than European by construction.

\section{Chargebacks}
\label{sec:bg-chargebacks}

When an issuer has refunded or credited a customer, it can try to recover the money through the
card network with a \emph{chargeback}. The issuer files the chargeback under one of the network's
reason codes; the network passes it to the acquirer, which acknowledges it and asks the merchant
whether to accept it or to contest it with evidence; the network's rules then decide which side
bears the loss. In practice a contested chargeback can go through several further rounds, under
names such as representment, pre-arbitration and arbitration, each with its own deadlines and
fees. This thesis folds those rounds into a single step, in which evidence is submitted and the
chargeback is then either won or lost. Chapter~\ref{ch:requirements} records that as a deliberate
cut. A chargeback is possible only where a merchant can be charged back: it exists for the card
regimes and not for a SEPA claim inside its eight weeks.

\section{What a dispute engine must therefore guarantee}
\label{sec:bg-guarantees}

The four regimes, summarised in figure~\ref{fig:regimes}, share a lifecycle, in which a complaint
is opened, investigated, settled and closed, and differ in which parts of that lifecycle exist.
From them follow the guarantees the rest of the thesis designs for. The engine must allow each
dispute exactly the steps its regime allows, and no others. It must run each regime's deadlines,
in business days where the law says business days, in the calendar of the institution that owes
them. It must move money once and only once, in the right direction, and be able to reverse a
credit where the regime provides for it. It must produce the written notices the customer is owed.
And it must keep a record of who did what, and when, that an auditor can trust.
